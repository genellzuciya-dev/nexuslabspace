How we work

The phases a project moves through, and biomimicry explained one layer at a time.

Our typical project phases

Frame the system, ask nature, prototype, and test in context.

Phase01
The step that matters most

Frame the system

What does this really need to do, and for whom?
Why this phase comes first

No one today is short on data. What is rare is the right question. This phase is what sets Nexus Lab Space apart. Before we ideate anything, we look at your system in context to find the points of tension and the leverage points, and make sure we are asking the right question with the right approach.

We restate your challenge as a function (shed heat, hold attention, move people, share resources) and map the system around it: who is involved, where the feedback loops are, and which constraints are fixed. A precise frame is what makes the biology useful later.

What we do

  • Functional diagnosis of the challenge
  • Systems and stakeholder mapping workshop
  • Constraint lock: materials, cost, regulation, timeline

You get

  • Challenge brief written as functions
  • Systems map with leverage points
  • Success criteria everyone signs off on
Phase02

Nexus asks nature

Which organisms and ecosystems already do this well?

The Nexus team goes searching for biological examples by function. Then we go look: field observation, macro photography, and data where it helps. What comes back to you is a lineup of the biological strategies we found, each one traced to its scientific source. The key is that we also abstract those insights into simple design principles your team can apply without a biology degree.

What we do

  • Literature search by function
  • Field and macro observation
  • Abstraction into transferable principles

You get

  • Biological strategy lineup: every solution we found in nature, with citations
  • Shortlist of design principles
  • Field documentation you can reuse
Phase03

Prototype in hand

What is the fastest physical version we can learn from?

Principles become things. Depending on the challenge that means a bench rig, a playable game, a material sample, or a room-scale experience. Building early exposes tolerances and human behavior that slides and simulations miss.

What we do

  • Concept options from different strategies
  • Rapid fabrication, electronics, and optics
  • Playtests and tabletop prototypes

You get

  • Working prototype
  • Concept comparison against your criteria
  • Test plan for the next phase
What your prototype looks like
Track A · Products

Something you can hold. A quick 3D print, a working electronics build, a material sample, or a playable tabletop version. If it is a product, it will be in your hands.

Track B · Social systems

Something you can see. An easy-to-read visual or dashboard that shows the leverage points, where to iterate, and the data you need to track.

When a challenge calls for it, we build custom AI tool sets and trackable dashboards, so your data works toward the answer instead of piling up.

Phase04

Test in context

Does it work where it has to live?

We take the prototype into the real setting: a classroom, a street, a factory floor, a festival. We measure against the criteria from Phase 01 and report what the data says, including when it disagrees with the original idea.

What we do

  • Field pilots with real users
  • Sensing, observation, and data analysis
  • Iteration on what breaks

You get

  • Findings report with measured results
  • Revised design
  • Go, adjust, or stop recommendation
What testing in context can look like
With a target group

A product in real hands. Students, players or customers use it while we watch.

In the environment

A field test on site. We measure where nature does the work, like water flow across a site.

Across an organization

A new way of working. A process or team structure piloted across several groups at once.

In public space

A pilot where people gather. A street, plaza or festival, watching how people move and respond.

As a soft launch

A small first audience. An exhibit or experience opened to a few visitors before the full opening.

With data and AI tools

A tool in daily use. Staff try the dashboard or AI tool, or we check a model against past data.

On the bench first

A controlled stress test. Durability and materials tested before anything goes into the field.

Phase05A
Typically Track A · Products

Hand off

Who keeps this alive after we leave?

Healthy ecosystems do not depend on one species. We package the work so your team or community can build, run, and adapt it: specifications, facilitation guides, and training. What we learned feeds a shared library for your next challenge.

What we do

  • Specifications and production support
  • Training for staff or facilitators
  • Roadmap planning

You get

  • Build-ready specs and a success plan for implementation and training
  • Trained internal owners
  • A reusable library of strategies
Phase05B
Typically Track B · Social innovation

Steward and iterate

What needs to change as the system grows?

Social systems keep changing after a pilot, so some partnerships should not end at a handoff. Nexus Lab Space stays on under a longer agreement, often one to five years, as the outside eye. From a zoomed-out view we watch how the system responds, find the leverage points, and guide each round of iteration with your team.

What we do

  • Regular field reviews and check-ins
  • Leverage point mapping as the system shifts
  • Iteration cycles run with your team

You get

  • A long-term partner with a zoomed-out view
  • An updated systems map each cycle
  • Progress measured against shared goals

What is biomimicry?

You don't need to learn every step at once. Peel back one idea at a time, at your own pace.

Biomimicry, explained one onion layer at a time.

Layer 1 of 6

Nature has already done the R&D.

Life on planet Earth has spent 3.8 billion years evolving ways to survive and thrive that are efficient, renewable, and fit for their context. Many of the challenges we face in design, sustainability, relations, communications and more have already been solved somewhere in the natural world.

Layer 2 of 6

Ask what it does, not what it looks like.

Instead of copying a shape, as biophilic design often does, we ask a function question: how does nature keep cool, store energy, or move water?

Not "make it look like a leaf," but "how does a leaf shed water?"

Layer 3 of 6

Every organism fits its place.

A strategy works because of its conditions: climate, materials, neighbors. Matching the conditions matters as much as matching the problem.

Desert plants solve heat differently than rainforest plants.

Layer 4 of 6

Find the strategy underneath.

Look past the organism to the mechanism that actually does the work.

The hummingbird's green comes from layered structural color, not pigment.

Layer 5 of 6

Turn it into a principle.

Describe the strategy in plain terms any team can use, no biology degree required.

A gecko's grip, as a principle: many tiny contacts add up to a strong hold that still lets go easily.

The core

Our specialty: unlikely inspiration.

The most useful answers often come from where no one thought to look: a goose joining a flock, the skin of a desert leaf, the feathers of a hummingbird. Finding those connections, and making them work, is what Nexus Lab Space does.
